Title of the poem

jane · 2026

it always rains when i visit grandma’s— heat so thick i can’t even escape its breath in the shower: two rinses each day & still it’s suffocating. grandma says she’s selling the house, tired of accompanying its wood. more precisely, she tells my mom, who tells my sister, who finally tells me. but don’t worry, my sister says, she won’t sell it until you graduate. because her friend once said the house has good luck, i learn to let luck take credit for my success, determined not to be the subject of doorway gossip i listen to, knowing there’s a short list of colleges my korean relatives know

by name. my sister loves the house enough to collect all our pictures that i have no memory of: me at one, merciless as i yank the tail of my grandma’s once-white dog. one from the year after we moved, my tiny hand tucked into grandpa’s, his smile bright enough to capture a love i remember only through these glossy photos. how do you tell your mother you can’t love a man you can’t remember?

he hated mess, long hair. he loved my sister because she knew how to count before she should’ve. grandpa, i’m three months away from replacing my spine, a spine in some twisted way, that was once your spine because what is now mine was yours & you live inside me in places i don’t know how to reach.

sometimes, I pray to him: grandpa, if you’re there, please let me get an a+ on my chem test & grandpa, if you’re there, please tell me whether i’m going to die soon— because then i’ll know when to cut

my hair short. when i am sixteen, mom finally tells me how you died. this is how i learn that one summer day you walked down the same path i have learned by heart, a downward slope through the farms his neighbors cultivated. i learn in fifteen minutes, anaphylaxis will split your heart open, let the life stream out like toddlers wobbling towards a playground, blissfully unaware of death and what it takes.

in fifteen minutes, i can finish a bowl of kimchi and purple rice. in fifteen minutes, i can walk the trail to his grave and return, dry-eyed. in fifteen minutes, my mom finishes telling me a story.
